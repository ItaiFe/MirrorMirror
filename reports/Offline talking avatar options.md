# Bake the bronze face offline, composite it live

The best option for Mirror, Mirror is a **layered 2D sprite face with light procedural warping**, rendered on the Jetson's CPU in the existing pygame loop. Its mouth, eyelid and expression patches would be produced once, offline, from the single AI image. A neural portrait animator (LivePortrait) runs on a rented desktop GPU to make those patches, and **Piper's built-in phoneme alignments** plus the existing RMS envelope drive them at runtime. No published benchmark shows any neural talking-head model running on a Jetson Orin, and the lightest real-time model, LivePortrait, needs about 15 ms per frame on an RTX 4090. Scaled to an Orin Nano that already shares its GPU with Gemma 3 4B, Whisper and TTS, it lands at an estimated 2–5 fps, so on-device neural animation is ruled out. The recommended path instead uses no GPU at all. Estimates: well under 5 ms per frame at 252×252, roughly 10–50 MB of RAM, zero added latency beyond TTS buffering, and identical behaviour for Hebrew and English, because lip timing comes from the TTS phonemes, not from audio recognition. The bronze, black-background medium works in this approach's favour. A pure-black mouth cavity reads as a void into the mirror, small warps hide at LED resolution, and rigid metal suits restrained motion better than rubbery deformation. A Live2D puppet via the new aarch64 `live2d-py` wheel is the strongest runner-up. A depth-map "bronze relief" with a moving-light metal shader is the most visually ambitious upgrade. Full 3D heads and on-device neural models rank last for this hardware.

## Six options ranked for a shared Orin Nano and a bronze trickster

The core constraint is not resolution, which is tiny, but **contention**. The Orin Nano's single Ampere iGPU and 8 GB of unified memory already host an LLM, an STT model and TTS, leaving roughly 1–2 GB and a GPU that llama.cpp keeps busy during generation. Every option below is judged on how little of that shared budget it takes and how well it serves a sculpted metal face. All Jetson performance figures are engineering estimates, because none of the research turned up measured numbers on this board for any approach. Plan a one-afternoon on-device benchmark before committing.

| Rank | Approach | Expected FPS on Orin Nano (shared) | Added latency | Memory | GPU use | Licensing | Effort (est.) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | Layered 2D sprite bank (offline-harvested mouth/eye patches) + small cv2.remap warps, Piper-alignment visemes | 30+ (CPU, ~1–5 ms/frame est.) | ~0 beyond TTS | ~10–50 MB | none | Piper GPL-3.0 (already used); LivePortrait MIT, offline only | 4–7 days |
| 2 | Pure procedural warp of the one image (jaw hinge, lid warp, black cavity), RMS-driven | 30+ (CPU) | ~0 | <10 MB | none | none new | 1–2 days |
| 3 | Live2D puppet via `live2d-py` 1.0.0 in a pygame OpenGL window | 30+ (sliver of GPU, est.) | ~0 | tens of MB | minimal | MIT binding; Live2D SDK license, "events" need contact | +2–5 days on top of asset work |
| 4 | 2.5D depth relief: displaced plane + PBR metal shader in moderngl | 30 (est., small GL cost) | ~0 | tens of MB | small, jitter risk | Depth Anything V2 Small Apache-2.0 | 1–2 weeks |
| 5 | Full 3D bust from image-to-3D + blendshapes, moderngl/Godot | 30 (est.) | ~0 | tens of MB | small, jitter risk | varies by generator | 2–4 weeks |
| 6 | On-device neural talking head (FasterLivePortrait TRT, Ditto, MuseTalk, Wav2Lip) | ~1–5 (est.) | seconds | 0.25 GB to several GB | heavy, competes with Gemma | mixed, several NC | high, unproven on aarch64 |

The ranking turns on one observation. The face needs to look alive more than it needs to look photoreal, and almost everything that makes it look alive can be **precomputed into pixels** on a big GPU, then replayed cheaply. Options 1 and 2 do exactly that. Options 3–5 move the same idea into a GPU renderer for parallax or relighting. Option 6 tries to do the expensive generation live on the wrong machine.

## Neural talking heads are offline tools, not runtime engines, on this board

The fastest open portrait animator is LivePortrait. Its per-frame breakdown on an RTX 4090 is 0.82 ms for the appearance extractor, 0.84 ms for the motion extractor, 7.59 ms for the SPADE generator and 5.21 ms for the warping module: **about 14.8 ms per frame and roughly 500 MB of FP32 weights** ([LivePortrait speed.md](https://github.com/KlingTeam/LivePortrait/blob/main/assets/docs/speed.md)). The TensorRT port FasterLivePortrait reaches "30+ FPS" on an RTX 3090 ([FasterLivePortrait README](https://huggingface.co/spaces/AndroidGuy/FasterLivepotrait/blob/main/README.md)). The Orin Nano is roughly 10–30x slower than those cards. On that scale an estimated **2–5 fps with the GPU to itself** is the ceiling, and it gets worse while Gemma decodes. The generator and warper are 87% of the cost, so feeding keypoints procedurally saves almost nothing. Memory is not the blocker; compute is. The deployment path is also rough. A 2026 paper found ONNX Runtime's TensorRT provider stalling at session setup on an Orin Nano, which forced offline device-specific engine builds ([arXiv 2608.17874](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.17874)). No FasterLivePortrait build for JetPack or Orin was found.

Audio-driven models are strictly heavier, because each adds a speech encoder that would duplicate Whisper. Ditto layers HuBERT plus a motion diffusion model on LivePortrait-style rendering. Its prebuilt TensorRT 8.6 engines and custom `grid_sample_3d` plugin target Ampere desktop GPUs ([Ditto GitHub](https://github.com/antgroup/ditto-talkinghead)). MuseTalk does "30fps+ on an NVIDIA Tesla V100" using Whisper features and the SD VAE ([MuseTalk GitHub](https://github.com/TMElyralab/MuseTalk)). LatentSync needs 6.5 GB of VRAM ([LatentSync GitHub](https://github.com/bytedance/LatentSync)). The 2026 streaming models are impressive on desktops but still far beyond the Nano. SoulX-FlashHead Lite claims 96 FPS on a 4090 with a 1.3B-parameter model ([SoulX-FlashHead arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.07449)), and AVTR-1 claims real time on an RTX 3070/4060 Ti class GPU ([AVTR-1 arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.22913)). The only "talking avatar on Jetson AGX Orin" project found ran STT, LLM and TTS on the Orin but rendered on a separate RTX PC ([Hackster](https://www.hackster.io/shahizat/ai-powered-photorealistic-talking-avatar-abac41)).

Material and licensing add two more reasons to keep these models offline. Lip-inpainting models (Wav2Lip, MuseTalk, LatentSync) paste learned human-mouth texture into the face, which risks pink lips and teeth on a bronze sculpture. Keypoint-warping models move the existing texture. JoyVASA's identity-agnostic motion "animate[s] animal faces seamlessly" ([JoyVASA arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.09209)), which suggests they generalise beyond photoreal humans, though no source tests bronze. Wav2Lip is non-commercial ([Wav2Lip README](https://huggingface.co/camenduru/Wav2Lip/resolve/main/README.md)). LivePortrait's code and weights are MIT, but its bundled InsightFace detectors "cannot be used commercially" ([Replicate fofr/live-portrait](https://replicate.com/fofr/live-portrait/readme)). That restriction disappears if you crop the one fixed image by hand and run LivePortrait only during asset production. **LivePortrait is excellent as an asset factory**: its separate eye- and lip-retargeting modules accept scalar "eyes-open" and "lip-open" ratios ([LivePortrait paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.03168v2)), exposed as `input_eye_ratio`/`input_lip_ratio` in hosted versions ([Sieve LivePortrait](https://sievedata.com/functions/sieve/liveportrait)). That is exactly the control needed to sweep a clean mouth-open ladder and blink sequence from one still.

## Puppets and 3D heads buy parallax at a steep rigging price

**Live2D is the most practical full-puppet runtime, and it became viable only recently.** `live2d-py` 1.0.0 shipped on 2026-09-30 with a `manylinux_2_28_aarch64` wheel ([PyPI files](https://pypi.org/project/live2d-py/#files)). It is an MIT-licensed binding that renders into "any window that has an OpenGL context", with a pygame example and lip-sync support ([live2d-py GitHub](https://github.com/Arkueid/live2d-py)). It needs Python ≥3.11, while Ubuntu 22.04 on JetPack ships 3.10, so plan a uv/deadsnakes interpreter. Two open questions remain. Live2D staff once said the Native SDK had no Linux aarch64 Core and later promised only an *experimental* one ([Live2D community](https://community.live2d.com/discussion/comment/3613/)), so nobody has confirmed the wheel works on a Jetson. Licensing is free for individuals and small businesses, but "Gaming Machines, Events, Runtime, etc." must be discussed with Live2D, and the license must be completed at least a month before release ([Live2D SDK License](https://www.live2d.com/en/sdk/license/)). An installation sits squarely in that grey zone, so send an email early. The real cost is labour: cutting the AI image into lips, mouth interior, eye whites, lids and brows, then rigging it in Cubism Editor. Live2D's payoff over option 1 is proper occlusion and small head-turn parallax. For a mostly frontal mask behind acrylic, that is a modest gain for days of work. The alternatives are weaker. Inochi2D is BSD-2 but explicitly not production-ready and has no Python path ([inox2d GitHub](https://github.com/Inochi2D/inox2d)). Spine needs a paid editor license to use its runtimes ([Spine Editor License](https://en.esotericsoftware.com/spine-editor-license)). Rive's MIT C++ runtime has no Python binding ([Rive C++ docs](https://rive.app/docs/runtimes/cpp/overview)).

A full 3D bust is tempting for real bronze lighting, but every link in its chain is long. Image-to-3D tools can produce a base mesh. TRELLIS code is MIT, but it depends on NVIDIA research-licensed nvdiffrast ([TRELLIS issue #298](https://github.com/microsoft/TRELLIS/issues/298)). Hunyuan3D's community license excludes the EU, UK and South Korea ([Hunyuan3D-2 LICENSE](https://huggingface.co/tencent/Hunyuan3D-2/blob/main/LICENSE)). The meshes then need retopology, a cut mouth, eyelid separation and blendshapes, for example Faceit's semi-automatic 52 ARKit shapes at about $78 ([Superhive: Faceit](https://superhivemarket.com/products/faceit)). NVIDIA's open Audio2Face-3D would be the natural dense driver, but it requires CUDA ≥12.8 and TensorRT ≥10.13 on Windows/Linux x86, with no Jetson mention ([Audio2Face-3D SDK](https://github.com/NVIDIA/Audio2Face-3D-SDK)). Rendering itself would be cheap: the Orin exposes OpenGL 4.6 ([ePower listing](https://www.epower.co.th/product/54742-71268/jetson-orin-nano-4gb)), and moderngl supports EGL contexts ([moderngl docs](https://moderngl.readthedocs.io/en/5.8.2/techniques/headless_ubuntu_18_server.html)). Avoid a browser renderer. NVIDIA staff said WebGL was "not supported" on JetPack 5 Chromium ([NVIDIA forum](https://forums.developer.nvidia.com/t/webgl-support-chromium-firefox-on-jetsons/224585)). The unmeasured risk for any GL route is frame jitter while llama.cpp kernels share the iGPU.

The **2.5D relief** is the 3D option worth keeping on the roadmap. Run monocular depth on the AI image offline with Depth Anything V2 Small, the only Apache-2.0 size, since Base/Large/Giant are CC BY-NC ([Replicate: depth-anything-v2](https://replicate.com/chenxwh/depth-anything-v2)). Then render a displaced, normal-mapped plane with a metallic shader and moving lights. On a two-way mirror where only lit pixels show, grazing light sweeping across bronze would be the most striking "emerging from darkness" effect available. Its weakness is the mouth: per-viseme depth maps taken from separately generated images will disagree, so talking still falls back to the same 2D patch technique as option 1. Build option 1 first; the relief shader can later replace its base layer without changing the animation logic.

## Piper already emits the lip-sync timeline, in both languages

The most important finding for lip sync is that **the TTS you already run can output per-phoneme timing at almost no cost**. In piper1-gpl, `PiperVoice.load(..., include_alignments=True)` patches the ONNX voice in memory. Each `AudioChunk` then carries `phoneme_alignments` entries with a `phoneme` and a `num_samples`, computed from the duration tensor times the hop length ([piper1-gpl voice.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/OHF-Voice/piper1-gpl/main/src/piper/voice.py)). Voices can also be patched permanently with `python3 -m piper.patch_voice_with_alignment model.onnx` ([ALIGNMENTS.md](https://github.com/OHF-Voice/piper1-gpl/blob/main/docs/ALIGNMENTS.md)). Version 1.6.0 added both in-memory patching and a Nakdimon-based Hebrew phonemizer ([piper1-gpl releases](https://github.com/OHF-Voice/piper1-gpl/releases)). Piper has also accepted raw phoneme input (`[[ ... ]]`) since v1.3.0, so Phonikud's phonemes should flow through the same alignment output. That last point is an inference that still needs testing on your actual Hebrew voice. Because alignments are reported per phoneme, a single phoneme-to-mouth table serves Hebrew and English alike. Both map onto Rhubarb's articulation-based set: A closed (p b m), B clenched (most consonants), C open (e, ɛ), D wide (a), E rounded (o), F puckered (u, w), G f/v, H l, X rest ([Rhubarb README](https://github.com/DanielSWolf/rhubarb-lip-sync)). Hebrew's ʁ and χ fall into B/C.

Two cautions keep this honest. The feature is marked experimental, and if phoneme IDs do not match, alignments come back `None` with "Phoneme alignment failed" logged ([voice.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/OHF-Voice/piper1-gpl/main/src/piper/voice.py)). A Piper fork also documented durations taken before `ceil()`, which made timings about 25% short and caused drift that grew over long sentences ([piper-plus #653](https://github.com/ayutaz/piper-plus/issues/653)). So rescale each chunk's summed alignment to its actual PCM length, anchor each sentence to the samples actually written, and **keep the existing RMS envelope** as both an intensity layer (mild versus wide variant of each viseme) and a full fallback. Rhubarb is MIT-licensed and accurate for English, but it is batch-only. Its language-independent "phonetic" recognizer, the only one usable for Hebrew, is "usually less precise" ([Rhubarb README](https://github.com/DanielSWolf/rhubarb-lip-sync)). It adds latency and CPU load that Piper's alignments make unnecessary. The amplitude-only fallback should not stay the primary driver: a MagicMirror community moderator warned that plain open/close mouths look "silly" ([MagicMirror forum](https://forum.magicmirror.builders/post/32488)), and visemes are what fix that.

## The top pick, from one AI image to a living face

### Asset production runs once, on a rented GPU, in about two to four days

Start by locking the source image. Take a front-facing, evenly lit 1024 px render of the bronze trickster, mouth closed and eyes open, on pure black. Crop it by hand so no face detector is needed. On a rented GPU (RunPod, Colab, Replicate), load it into LivePortrait, for example through the ComfyUI-LivePortraitKJ wrapper, where lip retargeting can be set to 0 to start closed ([RunComfy node docs](https://www.runcomfy.com/comfyui-nodes/ComfyUI-LivePortraitKJ/LivePortraitProcess)). Produce three things. First, a **neutral hub frame** (lip ratio 0, eyes 1). Second, a **jaw-open ladder** by sweeping lip ratio from 0 to 1 in four or five steps, which supplies the B/C/D shapes at mild and wide intensities. Third, a **blink ladder** by sweeping eye ratio from 1 to 0 in four steps. A scalar lip ratio cannot make rounded or labiodental shapes (E, F, G, H). Get those by driving LivePortrait with a few seconds of webcam video of yourself mouthing each shape, keeping the peak frame of each. That same driving-video trick yields the trickster's expression set: a sly half-smile, a raised brow, a squint for "thinking". MuseTalk can serve as an alternative mouth source, since it is MIT-licensed and its weights are usable for any purpose ([MuseTalk GitHub](https://github.com/TMElyralab/MuseTalk)). Feed it a long Hebrew-plus-English TTS clip, then cluster the output mouth crops into 9–20 representative shapes. Watch for human teeth appearing. Plain image editors (Nano Banana, FLUX Kontext) are a last resort, because each separate call can reinterpret the patina and lighting.

Then post-process everything into one coordinate frame. Downscale to 252×252 and register each frame to the hub with sub-pixel alignment. Cut fixed, feathered masks for the mouth (about 120×80 px) and each eye. Colour-transfer every patch to the hub's statistics so highlights match, and paint the mouth interior **pure black** so it shows as a hole in the mirror. Feathers must fade into the hub's own pixels, not into black, or a dark halo appears on the LED panel. Optionally, generate two or three 3–5 s **idle loops** with Wan 2.2 first-last-frame-to-video. Use the hub frame as both ends so the loop closes ([RunComfy Wan 2.2 FLF2V](https://www.runcomfy.com/comfyui-workflows/wan-2-2-flf2v-first-last-frame-video-generation)), and mask the mouth back to the hub so the loop never fights the visemes. Finally, mark about 20–40 control points (jaw, lip corners, brows, skull anchors). Precompute small displacement fields for jaw hinge, brow lift, breathing scale and gaze, using MLS via `molesq` ([molesq GitHub](https://github.com/clbarnes/molesq)). Save them as `.npy`. The result is about 1 MB of mouth patches (27 × 120×80 RGBA ≈ 38 KB each), a handful of eye and brow patches, and optional idle loops at about 28 MB per 150-frame loop of raw 252×252 RGB. That is arithmetic, not a measurement.

| Asset | Count | Source | Size (raw) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hub frame + face alpha mask | 1 | LivePortrait neutralised still | ~0.25 MB |
| Mouth patches A–H, X × mild/wide | 15–27 | LivePortrait lip sweep + driving video | ~1 MB |
| Eye patches (open/half/closed, 2–4 glints or gaze offsets) | 6–10 | LivePortrait eye sweep | <0.5 MB |
| Brow/expression patches (sly smile, squint, raised brow) | 3–6 | LivePortrait driving video | <0.5 MB |
| Warp fields (jaw, brow, breathe, gaze) | 4–8 | molesq MLS, offline | ~0.5 MB each |
| Idle loops (optional) | 2–3 | Wan 2.2 FLF2V, mouth masked | ~28 MB each |

### The runtime is a CPU compositor that slots into the existing face sink

At runtime the new renderer replaces the procedural pygame drawing behind the same face interface the conversation controller already uses. Each frame at 30 fps it takes the current base (hub or idle-loop frame) and builds a remap grid as identity plus a weighted sum of the warp fields. It applies one `cv2.remap` with `borderValue=0` and warps the face alpha mask with the same grid, so edge halos cannot turn into dim grey pixels. It then alpha-blends the current eye, brow and mouth patches. At 252×252 these are a few NumPy multiply-adds and small blits. The CPU cost is estimated at a few milliseconds per frame on the A78AE cores, but that is extrapolated from desktop and old ARM data, not measured on a Jetson. If OpenCV proves slow, NVIDIA VPI offers a hardware remap ([NVIDIA dev forum](https://forums.developer.nvidia.com/t/vpi-code-for-cv2-remap-cv2-warpperspective/351836)).

The four conversation states become target weight vectors with spring smoothing between them. **Idle** has slow breathing, random blinks every 2–6 s, occasional glances and a rare trickster smirk. **Listening** raises the brows and widens and centres the eyes. **Thinking** turns the gaze up and to the side, adds a squint, and optionally a faint warm glow in the mouth cavity. **Speaking** follows the viseme track. For speech, synthesise with alignments, rescale them to the PCM length, and map phonemes to shapes. Schedule them against the audio playback clock, leading by one or two frames, with a two-frame minimum hold and one-to-two-frame crossfades. Insert B between distant shapes such as A and D. RMS selects the mild or wide variant and adds a small rigid jaw-hinge offset, at most about 8–12 px, because larger warps make metal look rubbery. If alignment fails for a voice, the controller drops to RMS-quantised openness with closures shown as A. That is today's behaviour, only better looking.

## Conclusion

The question was posed as a choice among animation technologies. For this installation it is really a choice about where computation happens. The Orin Nano's GPU belongs to the conversation (Gemma, Whisper, TTS), so the face should cost nothing there. The way to get that is to spend GPU time once, offline, turning a single AI still into a small, internally consistent bank of bronze pixels. The neural models that cannot run live on the board become the best tools for building that bank. The TTS that already speaks both languages supplies the lip-sync timeline for free. The medium does the rest, because black reads as void, so the mouth needs no teeth or tongue.

Three things remain unproven and should be tested before building anything. First, check whether Piper's alignment patch works on your Hebrew (Phonikud) voice and stays in sync over long sentences. Second, check whether LivePortrait keeps the bronze texture intact on this particular sculpted face. Third, check whether a GL renderer (Live2D or the relief shader) holds frame times steady while llama.cpp is decoding. Each takes about an hour to test. Their results decide whether the long-term look stays a crisp 2D bank or grows into a relit relief. Either way, the asset pipeline and viseme controller described here carry over unchanged.
